## Week 3 · Kill your favorite idea

My favorite idea was that older patients with a standing treatment often have to make a clinic trip for a repeat prescription or a brief question, and often leave without it. I checked it against the interviews rather than treating every long wait or walk-in as proof. My grandmother's visit directly supported the repeat-prescription part: she waited about two hours with an appointment, left without the prescription, and had to make the trip again (log 01). Gocha and Zura also came in without appointments after calls went unanswered, but their visits were between-visit problems, not repeat-prescription renewals (logs 02 and 03); the doctor confirmed that brief questions can become walk-ins (log 04). I narrowed my claim: only one interview showed a patient leaving without a repeat prescription, so “often leave without it” is not established. This was a useful boundary to preserve, not a reason to overstate the evidence.

## Week 4 · The prototype is the conversation

My part of the prototype plan is bringing my father into the five-person test (log 05). His experience makes him a useful tester for the booking assumption: he called the clinic for three days, went in person to book, and waited two weeks for an appointment, but waited only 30 minutes on the visit day. The plan asks him to try finding a doctor and booking a future slot without help. That can show whether the demo flow is clear; it cannot show whether real clinic slots are available or whether online booking reduces waiting. I checked the test against his interview so the task is grounded in a real booking attempt, not a hypothetical need.
