## Week 1 · Act like an owner, not a role

My lane is discovery: interview quality and synthesis. I filled in my part of the contract and wrote three statements from things I had watched myself: my father walking out to the yard to check on a car, my cousin's flat arguing every month over who paid which bill, and my grandmother's monthly trip for the same prescription. The grandmother one is the one I had seen most often. It did not become the pick, but it is in the scorecard as the third shortlisted problem and it comes up in her log under question 1.

## Week 2 · Evidence over opinion

I asked in one interview and logged two. I interviewed my grandmother on 05.10 at her flat with Giorgi logging. She goes every month to renew the same prescription. On her last visit it took several tries to find an appointment that fit, the bus is about an hour each way, and she waited about two hours with an appointment before she had to leave without seeing the doctor. Giorgi watched for places where I answered for her. I was second logger for Gocha at the hospital café and for Zura at his flat, where I kept Giorgi on the calls when Zura jumped ahead to the trip, and made sure the six call-log dates were written down exactly. The thing I learned this week: having an appointment did not tell her when she would actually be seen, and she said that herself at the end, when I asked what I had missed.

## Week 3 · Kill your favorite idea

My favorite idea was that older patients with a standing treatment often have to make a clinic trip for a repeat prescription or a brief question, and often leave without it. I checked it against the interviews rather than treating every long wait or walk-in as proof. My grandmother's visit directly supported the repeat-prescription part: she waited about two hours with an appointment, left without the prescription, and had to make the trip again (log 01). Gocha and Zura also came in without appointments after calls went unanswered, but their visits were between-visit problems, not repeat-prescription renewals (logs 02 and 03); the doctor confirmed that brief questions can become walk-ins (log 04). I narrowed my claim: only one interview showed a patient leaving without a repeat prescription, so “often leave without it” is not established. This was a useful boundary to preserve, not a reason to overstate the evidence.

## Week 4 · The prototype is the conversation

I moved from discovery into the first version of our MediBook prototype: patients can find doctors and book visits, doctors can manage visits and treatment records, and hospital admins can manage appointment slots and see visits. I used the ER model to shape the app around the patient, doctor, hospital-admin, time-slot, appointment, treatment-case, consultation, and prescription relationships, with the backend supporting the booking and care-tracking flows. I also translated the discovery evidence into the five-part problem statement and mapped the alternatives people actually use—clinic visits, calling reception, relatives' help, and other workarounds—in the competitive landscape. The prototype makes our assumptions tangible enough to discuss and test; it does not yet prove that online slots match real clinic availability or reduce waiting.
