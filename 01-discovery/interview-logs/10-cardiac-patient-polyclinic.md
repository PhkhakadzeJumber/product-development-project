# Interview log: Dato, heart patient seen monthly by one cardiologist at a public polyclinic

**We are wrong if:** a patient whose medicine or result changed between visits tells us they got their doctor's answer on the first call or message, without a trip.

**Date:** 09.11.2026, morning · **Asker:** Luka · **Logger(s):** Giorgi · **Where / how:** in person, at a cafe near Dato's workplace in Kutaisi · **Length:** 30 minutes

Written up on 09.11.2026, the same day, from Giorgi's notes typed during the conversation (raw notes below).

**Who they are:** Dato, early 50s, a patient with a chronic heart condition. Referred by Zura ([log 03](03-diabetic-polyclinic.md)). Sees the same cardiologist at a public polyclinic once a month for check-ups and prescription renewals. Works at an electronics store. Not in this course. Spoke Georgian; translated by Luka.

**Context for readers:** Patient questions from interview script v2. He fits our ICP: an ongoing condition, one regular doctor at a public clinic, and a medicine that gets adjusted between visits.

## Warm-up

**1. How often do you usually need to visit a doctor or hospital?**

He goes once a month, usually around the 25th, to see his cardiologist for a routine check-up and to get his prescription slips renewed.

## Core (for patients)

**2. Walk me through the most recent time you needed care, from deciding to get help until you left or got an answer. What was the reason for it?**

His last planned visit was in late October, a standard check-up. The doctor listened to his heart, checked his blood pressure, and increased the dose of his beta-blocker to help with some palpitations.

**3. How long did it take to get to the clinic or hospital that time? How did you travel?**

About 20 minutes by Bolt taxi from his workplace.

**4. If you had an appointment, when were you actually seen compared with the scheduled time? If you had no appointment, how long did you wait after arriving? Did you leave without being seen or getting what you came for?**

For the planned visit his appointment was at 14:00, and he was called in at 14:30. He said a 30-minute delay is standard and no longer bothers him much.

**5. Since your last planned visit, tell me about the most recent time something changed or you had a question for your doctor. What did you do to reach them, how many attempts did you make, and what happened?**

Two weeks ago, after starting the higher dose of the beta-blocker, his resting heart rate dropped too low (he measures it at home) and he felt very dizzy and lethargic.

He has his doctor's personal mobile number. At 9:00 AM he sent a Viber message explaining the symptoms and checked his phone constantly. At 1:00 PM the "Seen" checkmarks appeared, but no reply came. At 3:00 PM he called the clinic registry; they told him the doctor was with patients and to try later. He called the registry again the next morning at 9:30 AM, and nobody picked up.

At 11:00 AM on that second day, still dizzy, he left work, took a taxi to the clinic and walked in without an appointment. He waited in the corridor for about an hour and a half before catching the doctor between booked patients.

**6. Tell me about the most recent question you chose to save for a routine visit. What happened while you waited, and what did you do when you finally got an answer?**

A few months ago he wanted to know whether he could take a magnesium and potassium supplement he had seen advertised on TV for heart health. He did not try to call. He wrote the name on a piece of paper, kept it in his wallet, and waited three weeks until his next monthly appointment to ask. The doctor said it was fine but unnecessary.

**7. Tell me about the last time you needed an appointment. How did you get it, and how long was it from first trying to book until the visit?**

He does not usually have to call to book. At the end of each monthly appointment the doctor writes down the date for next month, and Dato stops at the registry desk on his way out to lock it in.

**8. What did that episode cost in travel, waiting, money, work or other things you had to give up? Please walk me through the time and amounts.**

For the unplanned trip (the dizziness) he paid 8 GEL for a taxi there and 8 GEL back, 16 GEL in total. He had to leave his shift at the electronics store and lost about 3.5 hours of his workday.

**9. Thinking of the events we discussed, is there another specific moment about getting care that we should understand?**

When he caught the doctor in the hallway during the unplanned visit, the doctor was apologetic and, as Dato reported it, said: "I saw your message, but I had a room full of people and just forgot to type back by the end of the shift."

## Close

**10. Who else should we talk to about this, and who could introduce us?**

He offered to introduce us to his coworker, whose elderly mother has diabetes and who keeps trying to adjust her insulin doses over the phone.

**11. Could we come back when we have something to show you?**

Not recorded.

- **Why was that a problem?** — Pushed on why he did not call the doctor's mobile instead of sending a Viber message. Dato said calling the personal number feels "impolite" unless it is a life-or-death emergency, so he prefers a text or the official clinic desk.
- **When was the last time that happened?** — The side-effect episode two weeks before the interview, shortly after his late-October visit.
- **How long did it take?** — About 1.5 hours waiting in the corridor for an answer of less than a minute.
- **How many times did that happen?** — 1 Viber message, 2 calls to the registry, then 1 trip.

## Prediction check: did they raise the predicted problem before we mentioned it?

No, only after we asked question 5 about what happens when something changes between visits. Before that he described his routine monthly visits as fine.

## What they do about it today

- **Something changes between visits:** sends a Viber message to the doctor's personal number rather than calling it ("impolite" unless an emergency), then calls the registry, then goes in without an appointment after about a day.
- **Questions that are not urgent:** writes them on paper, keeps them in his wallet and asks at the monthly visit.
- **Booking:** the doctor sets the next date at the end of each visit, and he confirms it at the registry on the way out.

## Did this support or weaken the hypothesis?

- **Pattern 2 (patients cannot reach their doctor between visits):** Supports. A dose change caused side effects, he tried by message and by phone, did not get an answer, and made a trip. He did not get his answer on the first call or message, so this log does not meet our "we are wrong if".
- **Cost:** Supports. 16 GEL and about 3.5 hours away from work for an answer of under a minute.
- **Complication:** Pattern 2's root cause says all contact goes through the reception line, and earlier logs said doctors do not give out direct numbers ([03](03-diabetic-polyclinic.md), [04](04-polyclinic-doctor.md), [07](07-long-term-patient-clinic.md)). Dato had a direct line to his doctor and it still failed: the doctor read the message and, as Dato reported it, forgot to reply because the room was full. The bottleneck is also the doctor's time during a shift, not only the reception line.
- **Fits the other logs:** the registry told him the doctor was with patients (as the receptionist describes in [09](09-receptionist-polyclinic-followup.md)), and a booked visit runs about 30 minutes late ([03](03-diabetic-polyclinic.md): 20 to 30 minutes with an appointment).
- **Booking:** easy for him, like [02](02-heart-patient-ward.md), [03](03-diabetic-polyclinic.md) and [07](07-long-term-patient-clinic.md).

## What surprised me

Even with the doctor's personal mobile number and Viber, he did not get an answer and still had to make the trip. The bottleneck is not only the front desk's phone line; it is also the doctor's lack of time to write a reply during a shift.
