**Follow-up:** Can you remember the most recent time someone came in after being unable to get through? What happened?

Not asked or not answered.

**12. When a patient calls with a question for the doctor between visits, what do you do with it? What gets passed on and what does not?**

She said that when a patient has a question, they typically do not call but decide to come in, and that a larger share of patients do this than not. Her reason: doctors are rarely free or able to answer, and the ones who do talk spend time on it that they need for the other patients they treat during a typical day. She did not describe passing messages on to doctors or nurses. *To verify with Jumber: it is our reading that "does not call" refers to the patient, not to her calling the doctor.*

**13. When someone comes without an appointment and asks to be seen, how do you decide what to do with them?**

She did not describe how she decides. She said a patient without an appointment has to wait longer than booked patients: about 40 minutes to 1 hour, maybe a little more (her estimate).

**Follow-up:** Tell me about the last person this happened to. What did they do in the end, and roughly how long did they wait?

Not answered. She gave a general estimate, not a specific recent case.

**14. What is the hardest part of your day when the doctors are overloaded? What is the most recent specific example?**

She said that when the doctors are overloaded, reception is overloaded too, and there is a huge strain on the hospital at that time. Staff have a hard time meeting everyone's needs and keeping people from waiting a long time. No specific recent example was given.

**15. Is there anything about how appointments and repeat prescriptions work that patients most often misunderstand?**

She talked about prescriptions and buying medicine, not about appointment rules. Patients rarely buy something different from what was prescribed, but it happens sometimes. They usually do not buy it right after leaving; they buy some days after the visit. They need to have the prescription memorized. If they don't, they call their doctor or family members, because the slip is left at home and someone has to read it out loud or message it to them. She says this happens sometimes and called it messy. She did not explain the rules for repeat prescriptions or whether an in-person visit is required.

**Follow-up:** Can you walk me through the most recent repeat-prescription request? Did the patient get it that day? If not, what stopped it?

Not answered.

## Did this support or weaken the hypothesis?

- **Reception-line hypothesis:** Not tested. She did not mention unanswered calls, repeated attempts, or callbacks, and the missed-call question was not covered. This says nothing for or against it. It stays open for the next interview.
- **Waiting-time hypothesis:** Partly supports and partly complicates. Her estimate for patients without an appointment (about 40 min to 1 hour or more) fits our caveat that long waits happen mainly to people who come without one. Her estimate for booked patients (about 10 min) matches Zura's 20 to 30 minutes and is not a long wait. Both are estimates with no specific recent case.
- **Between-visit question hypothesis:** Supports the mechanism from the staff side. She says patients with a question mostly come in person because doctors are rarely free to answer, which matches the doctor's account in log 04. - **Repeat-prescription hypothesis:** Does not test it. She did not say why a patient must attend in person, and no patient failing to get a prescription was described. What she did describe (forgotten prescription details) is a related small-question problem, handled by phoning the doctor or family.
- **Counterevidence:** The ~10 min wait with an appointment points toward "appointments are seen promptly." It is a single estimate and does not outweigh the other logs, but it should be recorded.
