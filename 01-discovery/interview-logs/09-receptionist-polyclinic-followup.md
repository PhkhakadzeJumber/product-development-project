# Interview log: receptionist at the public polyclinic, follow-up to log 08 (clinic-staff side)

**We are wrong if:** a patient whose medicine or result changed between visits tells us they got their doctor's answer on the first call or message, without a trip.

**Date:** 08.11.2026, morning · **Asker:** Jumber · **Logger(s):** Luka, Giorgi · **Where / how:** In-person at the clinic reception desk (during a quiet period) · **Length:** 25 minutes

Second interview with the same receptionist as [log 08](08-receptionist-polyclinic.md), to cover what 08 missed: call volume, missed calls, callbacks, and how messages reach the doctor. Log 08 is unchanged. Written up on 09.11.2026, within 24 hours of the interview, from the raw notes taken during the conversation (below).

**Who they are:** The receptionist at the same public polyclinic in Kutaisi. Not in this course. Spoke Georgian; translated by Jumber.

**Context for readers:** The questions are the clinic-staff questions used in log 08, focused on the gaps it left open; they are not in interview script v2. The receptionist sits between the patients and the doctors, so she has a direct view of how patients try to get in touch, what happens when they fail, and the impact walk-ins have on the waiting room. 

## Warm-up

**1. How long have you worked at this registry, and what do you usually handle during a normal day?**

She has worked at the registry since 2022 (about 4.5 years). She handles booking appointments, answering the main clinic phone line, pulling physical paper files from the archives for the doctors, and managing the queue of patients who arrive in person. She described it as a high-stress position because she is the first person patients complain to when the schedule breaks down or the phones go unanswered.

## Core (for clinic staff)

**2. Walk me through the most recent busy day at the registry, from opening to closing. About how many calls came in, and how many people came in person to book or ask questions?**

She walked us through Monday of this past week. It is always the worst day because issues pile up over the weekend. She estimated (not counted) that they received over 100 calls before lunch alone. The phone rings constantly while she is simultaneously trying to register people standing right in front of her. She estimated (not counted) that on that Monday, about 15 to 20 people came in person without an appointment, not to get a full exam, but just because they needed to ask their doctor a quick question about a medication or a test result and couldn't get through on the phone. 

**3. What usually happens to a call when the line is busy or rings out? Is there a callback, a queue, or a log of missed calls?**

There is no automated queue or callback system, just a basic multi-line desk phone. The phone just rings out. If she is dealing with a patient in person, she often has to let it ring. She can see a missed call log on the phone's digital screen, but she ignores it when it's busy. "I can see the numbers piling up on the screen, but I have a line of sick people staring at me," she said. By 10:30 AM on Monday, she counted 18 missed calls before the log started overwriting older numbers. She does not have the time to call them back; she assumes they will either call again later or just show up.

**4. When a patient calls with a question for the doctor between visits, what do you do with it? What gets passed on and what does not?**

If a patient actually gets through and has a question about a changed medicine or a symptom, she tries to transfer them directly to the doctor's room. However, the doctors are usually seeing scheduled patients back-to-back. "I transfer the call, but ninety percent of the time the doctor doesn't pick up because they have someone on the couch." If they don't pick up, she takes a handwritten note on a scrap of paper and physically puts it on the doctor's desk when she drops off files. But because doctors are so busy, they rarely call the patient back the same day, and the paper notes often just pile up.

**5. Tell me about the most recent time someone came in after being unable to get through on the phone. What happened?**

She gave a specific example from this past Thursday. An older man whose blood pressure medication dosage was changed on Tuesday started feeling dizzy. He called the clinic three times on Thursday morning. The receptionist saw his number in the missed call log but couldn't answer because the morning rush was too heavy. Around 1:00 PM, the man arrived at the clinic in person by marshrutka (minibus). 

**6. What happened to him, and what happened to the other patients waiting?**

Since he didn't have an appointment, she told him he had to wait in the corridor until his doctor had a gap. He sat in the waiting area for just under 2 hours. Finally, around 2:50 PM, the doctor finished with a booked patient and the receptionist physically slipped the older man into the room before the next person could stand up. The doctor answered his question in less than two minutes (he just needed to lower the dose). However, squeezing him in pushed the 3:00 PM scheduled appointment to 3:15 PM, and the 3:15 PM appointment to 3:30 PM. The booked patients in the waiting room got angry and started knocking on the doctor's door.

**7. What is the hardest part of your day when the doctors are overloaded? What is the most recent specific example?**

She said the hardest part is dealing with the "two-minute walk-ins"—patients who travel all the way to the clinic and wait hours just to ask a tiny question because the phone system fails them. She hates seeing elderly patients waste money on travel and sit in a hallway full of sick people for something that should be a 30-second phone call. She also hates that fitting them in ruins the schedule for the people who actually followed the rules and booked in advance.

## Close

**8. Thinking about everything we've talked about, is there anything important about dealing with doctors or hospitals that I didn't ask you about?**

She mentioned repeat prescriptions. Patients often get home, lose the slip, or forget what the doctor told them to buy. They try to call, fail, and end up coming all the way back to the clinic. "They spend three lari on a minibus and wait two hours just to have the doctor write the same name on a new piece of paper," she noted.

**9. Who else should we talk to about this?**

She suggested we talk to the head nurse, as she is sometimes the one trying (and failing) to return the missed calls from the paper notes at the very end of the shift. Next question for her: how many of the paper notes get a call back, and how long after?

**10. Could we come back when we have something to show you?**

Not asked.

## Prediction check: did they raise the predicted problem before we mentioned it?

No, only after we asked. This was a direct follow-up aimed specifically at asking about missed calls, busy lines, and what happens between visits. 

## What they do about it today

- **Missed calls:** She ignores them when busy; leaves them in the phone log. Occasionally the head nurse tries to call back at the end of the day, but often they are abandoned.
- **Questions between visits:** She takes handwritten notes on scraps of paper and leaves them on the doctor's desk.
- **Walk-ins for quick questions:** She makes them wait in the hallway for hours until a scheduled patient finishes early or leaves a gap, then physically intercepts the doctor to squeeze the walk-in through, sacrificing the schedule.

## Did this support or weaken the hypothesis?

- **Pattern 2 (Patients cannot reach their doctor between visits):** Strongly supports. This is the exact mechanism we suspected. A patient's medicine changed, he had a side effect (dizziness), he attempted to call 3 times, failed because the clinic lacks capacity to answer/return calls, and was forced to make a trip.

- **Impact on doctors/clinic:** Supports. The unmet demand on the phones spills over into the physical waiting room, delaying scheduled patients and stressing the staff.

- **Strength of the evidence:** The dizzy man's story is second-hand (what she saw and what he told her), and most numbers are her estimates; the 18 missed calls is the one number she read off a screen.

- **Compared with log 08 (same person, 10.10):** Fits on waits (08: 40 minutes to an hour or more without an appointment, one case of 2.5 hours; here just under 2 hours) and on reception strain. Two differences to keep in mind: in 08 she said patients with a question mostly do not call but come in, while here she describes them calling first and coming in after the calls fail; and in 08 patients who forget a prescription call the doctor or family to read the slip, while here they come back to the clinic. Our reading is that both are true for different patients; the next interview with her or the head nurse should ask which is more common.

## What surprised me

That the actual "system" for relaying patient medical questions is writing them on loose scraps of paper and leaving them on a busy doctor's desk, and that doctors are too overwhelmed to call back, leaving the head nurse to attempt it (often unsuccessfully) after the clinic closes.
