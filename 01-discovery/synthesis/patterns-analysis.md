## Pattern 1: An appointment does not mean being seen on time — walk-ins taken in gaps push booked patients late

**Interviews behind it:** 01, 02, 03, 04, 08, 09, 10

1. Why does this happen? Reception sends walk-ins to sit outside the door and the doctor takes them in the gaps, so booked patients go in late. (04 logged mechanism; 02 watched three booked patients go in late; 09, from the reception desk: one walk-in slipped in at 2:50 PM made the 3:00 and 3:15 booked patients each start 15 minutes late; 10: booked for 14:00, called in at 14:30, and on his unplanned trip he was the walk-in, 1.5 hours in the corridor for a gap.)
2. Why do so many come without an appointment? They could not reach the clinic by phone first, so showing up is the fallback. (03: "Nothing happened. It rang. Nobody picked up." — 6 calls over 3 days; 02: 2 calls, no callback. 05 hit the same phone wall but booked in person instead of walking in, so his detail sits in Pattern 2.)
3. Why does phone contact fail? The doctor is seeing patients and gives out no mobile, so everything funnels via the reception/registry line. (04: does not give out mobile number; 03: mobile refused, "Only that the girl at reception decides everything. If she likes you, she passes the message. If not, nothing.")
4. Why take walk-ins in the gaps at all instead of turning them away? Anyone who looks unwell goes first and there is nobody else to send them to. (04: unwell first; 06: nurses prioritise who looks worst.)

**Root cause, one sentence:** Booked and unbooked demand share one corridor and one doctor with no buffer, so walk-ins that failed to get through by phone are absorbed in the gaps at booked patients' expense.

**Which of the answers above is a guess rather than a quote? (be honest; this becomes a question for your next interview):** Why 4's "nobody else to send them to" is clean in 06 (hospital queue outlasts the shift) but inferred for the polyclinic in 04. Added to script v2: what happens if you refuse a walk-in?

## Pattern 2: When something changes between visits, patients cannot reach the doctor by phone, so they keep calling for days or come in person

**Interviews behind it:** 02, 03, 05, 04, 07, 09, 10

1. Why does this happen? Lines are busy, ring with no pickup, and give no callback. (03: "Nothing happened. It rang. Nobody picked up."; 05: "I called three days, nobody picks up." — morning and afternoon, ~6 calls; 02: called twice, no callback; 07: 4 calls over 2 days, message passed through reception, no callback the first day; 09, the reception side: 18 missed calls on the desk phone's screen by 10:30 on a Monday, no queue and no callback, "I can see the numbers piling up on the screen, but I have a line of sick people staring at me."; 10: registry answered once, "the doctor is with patients, try later", and did not pick up the next morning.)
2. Why does that force a trip? The only fallback offered is showing up. (03, reception as reported: "Why do you keep calling, come in if it's urgent."; 05: "I closed the garage for an hour just to book."; 02 went Monday without an appointment. 07 is the exception that proves it: the doctor called back on day 2, so no trip.) 10 went in after about a day: "I lost three hours of work and sixteen lari on taxis just to hear him say 'stop taking it'."
3. Why is there no other channel? Doctor mobiles are withheld and the tried alternatives failed. (04: does not give mobile to avoid evening calls; 07: "normally they don't give you a direct number"; 03 tried mobile — refused, daughter searched online — nothing useful, 5th-floor nurse — would not comment on dose.) 10 is the one patient who did have the doctor's personal number, and it did not help: his Viber message was marked "Seen" at 13:00 and never answered, "I saw the two checkmarks on Viber, but the reply never came."
4. Why does a call that gets through still not reach the doctor? The doctor is with booked patients back to back, so a transferred call goes unanswered and the message becomes a handwritten note on the doctor's desk, rarely returned the same day. (09: "I transfer the call, but ninety percent of the time the doctor doesn't pick up because they have someone on the couch."; 03: "If she likes you, she passes the message. If not, nothing."; 10, the doctor as Dato reported it: "I saw your message, but I had a room full of people and just forgot to type back by the end of the shift.")

**Root cause, one sentence:** Between-visit contact depends on a reception line that cannot answer or return the calls and on a doctor who has no gap during the shift to answer a transferred call, a paper note or even a direct message, so any change forces a physical trip.

**Which of the answers above is a guess rather than a quote?** Until 09, "overloaded" for reception was inferred from patients' call counts (6 + 6 + 2 + 4) and 04's 31-patient day. The follow-up with the receptionist (09) backs it from the reception side: 18 missed calls by 10:30, read off the phone, and no callback. Still a guess: how many calls come in ("over 100 before lunch" and "ninety percent" of transfers failing are her estimates), and how many of the paper notes ever get a call back. Next interview: the head nurse she referred us to, who returns calls from the notes at the end of the shift. New since 10: whether a direct line to the doctor ever works between visits rests on one log, where it did not; the doctor's reason ("forgot to type back") is second-hand. Ask the next doctor how they handle patient messages during a shift.

## Pattern 3: A 3- to 10-minute visit costs half a day, bus fare, and lost work — and repeats every month

**Interviews behind it:** 01, 02, 03, 05, 09, 10

1. Why does this happen? Long travel each way plus a corridor wait buys only minutes with the doctor. (01: 1h bus each way + 2h wait, left unseen; 03: 40 min each way + ~2h wait for 3 minutes; 02: "I sat an hour and a half for five minutes."; 05: 30 min corridor for ~10 minutes; 10: 20 min taxi each way + 1.5h corridor for less than a minute.)
2. Why go in person for such short needs? The same repeat prescription and quick dose checks have no remote path. (01: same prescription monthly, "Every month half a day, and this month twice."; 03: "The tablet, you can't wait a month with 3.4." yet still had to come in; 09, the receptionist about patients who lose a prescription and cannot get through by phone: "They spend three lari on a minibus and wait two hours just to have the doctor write the same name on a new piece of paper.")
3. Why does it cost money beyond fare? Companions miss work and earners close up. (02: "My daughter had to miss work to bring me."; 03: "fifty, eighty lari I didn't make, who knows." plus 4 GEL transport and a closed shop morning; 05: "I closed the garage for an hour just to book." plus a lost working morning, and 2 weeks working half-strength: "If the book is full, you wait in pain."; 10: "I lost three hours of work and sixteen lari on taxis just to hear him say 'stop taking it'.", 8 GEL taxi each way and about 3.5 hours away from his shift.)

**Root cause, one sentence:** Routine and short-duration needs require physical presence, so travel plus queue plus lost work repeat on every cycle.

**Which of the answers above is a guess rather than a quote?** "No remote path exists" is supported by 03's three failed routes (mobile refused, internet nothing, nurse silent) and 01 asking Beka to call ahead, but we have not asked the doctor side what a remote renewal would break (e-system entry, examination rule). Next doctor interview: ask what stops a repeat prescription without a visit.

## Pattern 4: Doctors absorb more patients than the day fits by shortening visits and finishing paperwork after hours

**Interviews behind it:** 04, 06, 08

1. Why does this happen? Patient count exceeds slots and staff. (04: 22 booked + 9 walk-in = 31, usual 20–25 booked + 5–8 walk-in; 06: one doctor doing the work of two or three, Saturday 2 → 1 when one called in sick.)
2. Why does the day still run? Slots are cut and paperwork is deferred. (04: ~15 min cut to under 10, notes written next morning, left after 5 with no lunch: "By the afternoon I am rushing people I want to give more time to."; 06: skipped full examinations to triage, postponed discharge papers, 12 hours with one short tea break.)
3. Why does it land on mood, not just hours? Endless demand with no backup turns into exhaustion. (06: "You work tirelessly all day and still leave people waiting."; "Then you go home and think about quitting."; "I liked medicine. I no longer enjoy the job.")

**Root cause, one sentence:** Fixed hours plus open-door walk-ins plus short staffing leave rushing visits and after-hours notes as the only shock absorber.

**Which of the answers above is a guess rather than a quote?** Merging 04 (flow mechanics) and 06 (staffing/exhaustion) into one shock-absorber cause is an inference — each log alone supports its own half. The receptionist (08) echoed the flow half — she described reception straining when the doctors are overloaded — but said nothing about staffing, so the merge stays an inference until a nurse or a second doctor echoes the exhaustion half.

## Pattern 5: Questions that are not urgent are held until the next routine visit instead of being asked between visits

**Interviews behind it:** 03, 07, 10

1. Why does this happen? Getting an answer between visits costs days of calls, so only an urgent change seems worth it. (03: 6 calls over 3 days for a sugar of 3.4, but the spring test result waited: "It wasn't urgent."; 07: 4 calls over 2 days, "You don't want to keep calling them"; 10: "If I'm not dying, I just write it down on paper and wait for the twenty-fifth of the month.", a supplement question kept in his wallet for three weeks.)
2. Why does holding the question still hurt? The patient lives with not knowing until the visit. (07: "The difficult part was just not knowing.")

**Root cause, one sentence:** Between-visit contact is so costly that patients ration it to urgent changes and carry the rest as worry until the next visit.

**Which of the answers above is a guess rather than a quote?** We do not know whether a held question ever caused harm or an extra visit; neither 03 nor 07 was asked. 10 was asked: the saved question (a supplement) was answered "fine but unnecessary" at the visit, so no harm in that one case. Keep asking: "What happened with the last question you saved for your visit?"

---

**Strongest pattern (most interviews, clearest root cause):** Pattern 2 — when something changes between visits, patients cannot reach the doctor by phone, so they keep calling for days or come in person.

**Why it is the strongest, in two sentences, citing log numbers:** It has the most interviews behind it (02, 03, 04, 05, 07, 09, 10), with counted calls and call logs shown: 2 calls with no callback (02), 6 calls in three days on the phone's call log (03), 4 calls over two days (07), the doctor's walk-ins with two-minute questions they could not ask by phone (04), 18 missed calls on the reception phone's screen by 10:30 on a Monday (09), and a Viber message to the doctor's own number marked "Seen" with no reply plus two registry calls, shown on the phone (10). It also explains most of Pattern 3: Gocha and Zura paid the half day only because their calls went unanswered and they came without an appointment, while with an appointment Zura waits 20 to 30 minutes and calls it normal (02, 03).
